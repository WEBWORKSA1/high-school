📝 How to Take Better Notes: Cornell, Outline & Mapping

How to take notes that actually help you learn — the Cornell method, outlining, mind mapping, digital vs handwritten, and how to review notes for tests.

📅 Updated October 2026✍️ High.School Editorial Team✅ Editorial policy

Good notes do two jobs: they capture key ideas during class, and they become a study tool afterward. The method matters less than what you do with notes later — but the right format makes reviewing much easier.

The Cornell method

Divide the page into three areas: a narrow cue column on the left, a wide notes column on the right, and a summary strip at the bottom. Take notes on the right during class. Afterward, write questions in the cue column and a 2–3 sentence summary at the bottom. To study, cover the right side and answer the cue questions.

Outline method

Use headings and indented bullets for main ideas and supporting details. Best for well-organized lectures and textbooks.

Mapping

Put the central idea in the middle and branch out. Great for subjects with many connections (biology systems, history causes, literature themes).

Handwritten vs digital

Typing is faster, which tempts people to transcribe word-for-word. Handwriting forces you to summarize, which helps understanding. If you type, deliberately paraphrase. Either way, keep notes organized by date and unit.

The 24-hour rule

Review notes within a day: fill gaps, add questions, and quiz yourself. This turns notes into retrieval practice.

Frequently asked questions

Should I copy everything the teacher says?

No. Capture main ideas, examples, and anything the teacher repeats or writes on the board — those are likely test material.

How do I take notes from a textbook?

Skim headings first, read a section, close the book, then write notes from memory and check.
