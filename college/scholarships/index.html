🏆 Scholarships: Find, Track & Win

How to find and win scholarships in high school — a free deadline tracker, scholarship alerts, where to search, how to win more and how to spot scams.

📅 Updated October 2026✍️ High.School Editorial Team✅ Editorial policy

Scholarships are free money for college that you don’t repay. They come from colleges, companies, foundations, community groups and governments. The students who win the most aren’t always top of the class — they’re the ones who apply consistently, including to small local awards with fewer applicants.

Where to find scholarships

  • Your colleges: merit scholarships are often automatic with admission; check each college’s aid page.
  • Your school counselor’s list: local awards from businesses, credit unions and civic clubs.
  • Your state: many states fund merit and need-based grants.
  • Employers and unions: parents’ employers often sponsor awards.
  • Identity and interest groups: heritage, faith, first-generation, major-specific, athletic and arts organizations.
  • Free national search sites — never pay to apply or to “guarantee” a scholarship.

How to win more

  1. Apply monthly from junior year onward.
  2. Keep a master essay bank and adapt it — many prompts overlap.
  3. Follow instructions exactly; incomplete applications are discarded first.
  4. Ask for recommendation letters early and send thank-you notes.

Scam warning: legitimate scholarships never charge application fees or ask for bank details to “release” an award.

Students can also enter High.School’s own contests.

Frequently asked questions

When should I start applying for scholarships?

Some scholarships accept students as young as 9th grade, but most open in junior and senior year. Start tracking in 11th grade and apply monthly.

Are scholarship search sites legit?

Reputable search sites are free. Never pay a fee to apply for a scholarship or for a service that guarantees awards.

Do small scholarships matter?

Yes. $500–$1,000 local awards have fewer applicants, so your odds are higher — and they add up.
